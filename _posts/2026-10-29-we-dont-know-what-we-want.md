---
title: Do not throw your pearls before pigs
tags: [ai, writing]
---


Poteto recently [asked on X](https://x.com/poteto/status/2111321176769442251?a=irEBxaybbb43vs4kNZs&s=20) if chat is the final form of agent UI. Over 70% of respondents said no. The majority seems to envision some neural-link type, brain-agent connected interface, where our every thought is legible to our AI “companion” and instantly executable. I think there’s an implicit assumption that by doing this we will be able to build whatever we truly want because we will be unconstrained by the low throughput bit rate of human language.


However, language, or writing, isn’t the problem: the problem is that we don’t know what we want! When we don’t truly know what we want, writing becomes excruciatingly painful, because we are forced to transform our nebulous imaginary ideas into clearly defined, delimited, fully-developed concepts, where nothing is assumed and everything is apparent. In other words, we are forced to actually decide what we want.


What's the proof? Writers know more about what they want, about their imagination, than those who don’t write, even after having translated their mental ideas into “low-resolution” language. As Leslie Lamport says, writing forces us to expose the silent assumptions we hold. It’s these assumptions that are so often the wrong ones, and, until we are face-to-face with them, we are completely unaware. I am experiencing this very effect as I write: I must assume my reader cannot read my mind, and for you to understand fully what I am thinking, I must explain each part of what I am thinking very clearly, even the parts that, to me, may seem obvious. I must critically analyze each part of my idea, and by doing so, I learn a lot about what I haven’t fully thought-out. If writing were hard because it constrained my ideas, then the opposite would be true: I would finish the process understanding less, having been forced to relegate my “mental understanding” into lower resolution mere language.


If we do not write what we want, AI will auto-complete it for us, using its own criteria, which, in fact, is no criteria at all. It is simply the weights it learned from its reinforcement learning post-training phase, in some generic test environment setup by its model provider in SF; in other words, extremely disconnected from the particularities of our own minds.


The AI industry, through anthropomorphic verbs like “thinking”, API endpoints like “responses” and interfaces like “Chat”, has brainwashed us into believing that AI can intuitively understand us and, effectively, “just get it” when we direct it towards some poorly specified task or idea.


But this is just a marketing-ploy to get us to share an ever increasing amount of context about our work and personal life with the model providers and chat interfaces. They want us to feel comfortable doing so. It turns out that we, as humans, feel much more comfortable sharing this information with other individuals capable of empathy, rather than algorithms running in far-away cloud servers.


Truly, the models only “get it” to the extent that, in their post-training reinforcement learning phase, they have been shown situations where the user’s intent is equivalent to ours. So, critically, even if our idea is nuanced in a novel way that no-one has ever thought of, if we relegate its nuances to an AI expecting it to pick up on these nuances, it will instead funnel the idea back to the nearest example it saw in its post-training. All the subtlety and novelty of our idea is lost, and since we didn’t do the work to think it through fully, we aren’t even aware of what we lost. “Do not give dogs what is holy, and do not throw your pearls before pigs, lest they trample them underfoot and turn to attack you” applies poignantly here as well.


When we relegate thinking to AI, we end up hill-climbing to just local maxima: whatever AI has seen in post-training. But there remains to be found the global maximum. We must not surrender it. Getting there requires human-inspiration. It requires treasuring the fact that we have ideas that AI will never be able to reach. Only we can do this.
